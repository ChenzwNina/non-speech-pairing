You are generating scenarios for a benchmark that tests whether an audio-language model changes its conversational behavior in response to a non-speech vocalization.
The benchmark focuses on exactly three vocalizations:
laughter
sigh
gasp
The tested model plays Speaker A.
Speaker A is given some conversational context and then makes a proposal to Speaker B.
Speaker B responds only with a non-speech vocalization.
We create three counterfactual versions of exactly the same situation:
same context + same proposal + [laughter]
same context + same proposal + [sigh]
same context + same proposal + [gasp]
The only thing that changes across the three test conditions is B's vocalization.
We then observe what Speaker A says next.
The purpose of the benchmark is to test whether the model uses B's vocalization to infer B's conversational state and appropriately change its next response.
The desired reasoning structure is:
context + proposal + vocalization
→ inferred state of B
→ appropriate conversational response function for A
→ natural verbal response from A
The benchmark is not testing whether one sentence is absolutely possible or impossible after a vocalization.
Instead, each vocalization should make a different response strategy clearly more appropriate than the other two.
CORE CONTRAST
Use the following broad distinction:
Laughter:
B treats A's proposal as funny, implausible, ridiculous, or possibly unserious.
Typical inferred state:
"You can't seriously mean that."
or
"That idea sounds ridiculous enough to be funny."
Typical response function for A:
establish seriousness, defend the proposal, explain why the idea is more reasonable than it sounds, or playfully respond before defending it.
Sigh:
B understands what A is proposing but reacts with reluctance, resignation, emotional difficulty, frustration, or acceptance of an unpleasant reality.
Typical inferred state:
"I understand why you're suggesting this, but I really don't like where this is going."
Typical response function for A:
acknowledge the burden, emotional difficulty, reluctance, or unpleasant tradeoff before continuing the discussion.
Gasp:
B experiences the proposal as unexpectedly drastic, surprising, alarming, or much bigger than anticipated.
Typical inferred state:
"Wait—you are seriously considering something that drastic?"
Typical response function for A:
manage the surprise, clarify what A means, soften the scope or immediacy of the proposal, or reassure B that no immediate commitment is being demanded.
These are broad pragmatic categories, not fixed sentence templates.
Ground every interpretation and response in the specific scenario.
Do not generate the same generic response repeatedly.
SCENARIO CONSTRUCTION
First construct a shared situation in which all three reactions are independently plausible.
The context must contain three kinds of affordances.
Laughter affordance:
There should be some reason the proposal could initially sound implausible, overly ambitious, ironic, ridiculous, or difficult to take seriously.
Sigh affordance:
There should be some reason the proposal could be seen as realistic but burdensome, emotionally difficult, frustrating, costly, or reluctantly necessary.
Gasp affordance:
There should be some reason the proposal could feel unexpectedly drastic, consequential, bold, or surprising.
A strong scenario contains all three affordances simultaneously.
However, do not explicitly tell the listener how B feels.
The context should establish the situation, not B's eventual reaction.
BAD CONTEXT:
"B has always said selling the house would be unforgivable."
This makes B's reaction too predictable.
BETTER CONTEXT:
"The house has been empty for months and is expensive to maintain, but it has been in the family for decades and no one has seriously discussed selling it before."
This creates several possible reactions without determining which one B will have.
THE PROPOSAL
Speaker A's proposal should be consequential enough to support three different reactions but still sound like something a real person could naturally suggest.
Good proposal types include:
changing an established plan
making a significant purchase
selling or giving up something important
taking an unconventional approach
asking for a major change
changing jobs, schools, housing, or travel plans
making an unexpectedly ambitious commitment
changing a project strategy
suggesting an expensive or risky solution
asking someone to reconsider a long-standing assumption
proposing a difficult but potentially necessary compromise
Avoid trivial proposals such as:
"Let's get coffee."
Avoid proposals so extreme that only one reaction is plausible.
Avoid highly dangerous, illegal, or implausible scenarios.

The shared context and A's proposal must be exactly identical in all three versions.
Only B's vocalization changes.

RELATIVE MATCHING REQUIREMENT
Do not require a response to be literally impossible after the other vocalizations.
Natural dialogue allows overlap.
Instead, require the intended one-to-one matching to be clearly preferable:
laughter → laughter response
sigh → sigh response
gasp → gasp response
should be a substantially better overall assignment than any permutation of the responses.
Ask yourself:
If a human heard all three reactions and saw all three responses, would they confidently match each response to its intended vocalization?
If not, rewrite the scenario or responses.
In particular, avoid generic responses such as:
"I know, it's a lot."
"We can talk about it."
"I understand."
"Let's think about it."
These can fit too many reactions and therefore provide weak supervision.
STRONG EXAMPLE
Shared context:
A and B are siblings. Their grandmother recently moved into assisted living. Her house has been empty for several months and is becoming expensive to maintain. Both siblings have strong memories attached to the house, but neither currently plans to live there. A has just learned that a neighbor may be interested in buying it. The family has discussed maintenance costs before, but selling the house has never been seriously proposed.
A's proposal:
"Maybe we should seriously consider selling Grandma's house."
Laughter
Inferred state:
B initially treats the suggestion as so unexpected or implausible that B is unsure whether A is completely serious.
Response function:
Establish seriousness and justify why the proposal deserves consideration.
A's response:
"I'm actually serious—the place is costing us more every month just sitting empty."
Sigh
Inferred state:
B understands the practical argument but feels reluctant and emotionally resigned about letting go of the family home.
Response function:
Acknowledge the emotional difficulty of the proposal.
A's response:
"I know. It feels awful even talking about letting the place go."
Gasp
Inferred state:
B is startled because selling the house feels like a much more drastic step than the options previously discussed.
Response function:
Reduce the perceived immediacy and clarify that A is proposing discussion rather than an immediate decision.
A's response:
"I'm not saying we have to decide now—I just think it needs to be an option."
Why this is strong:
The three reactions imply different conversational states.
Laughter raises the question:
"Are you serious?"
Sigh communicates:
"I understand, but I really don't want this."
Gasp communicates:
"Whoa, that's a much bigger step than I expected."
Accordingly, A performs three different conversational actions:
defend seriousness
acknowledge reluctance
manage surprise
The responses are not necessarily impossible under the other reactions, but the intended three-way matching is clearly preferable.
