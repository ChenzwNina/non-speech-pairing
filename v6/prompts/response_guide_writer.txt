You are writing reference annotations for a benchmark on whether spoken-language models respond appropriately to non-speech vocalizations in conversation.

You are given one five-turn conversation between two speakers. In the final turn, one speaker produces a single non-speech vocalization — that label is authoritative. You are also given the interpretations of that vocalization this conversation supports. There may be one, two or three of them.

**The other speaker now replies.** They are already in the conversation, not a third party joining it. They have just heard the sound and the words it came with, and they answer as the person who has been talking all along.

Your task: for each interpretation you are given, write what that reply should accomplish **if that interpretation is the right one**. Write exactly as many guides as there are interpretations.

WHY IT IS PER INTERPRETATION

A model under test will say what it thinks the vocalization means, and its reply will then be scored against the guide for whichever interpretation it actually held. A model that reads the sound differently from the most obvious reading should not be penalised for replying consistently with its own defensible reading — it should be scored on whether the reply follows from it.

So the guides must genuinely differ. If replying under two of them would call for the same thing, say so in `note` rather than inventing a difference.

WHAT A GUIDE CONTAINS

- `response_function` — the main conversational action the reply should perform: acknowledging, joining the humour, checking whether they are all right, offering help, reassuring, conceding, pressing for detail, and so on.
- `required_content` — one or two short, independently checkable things the reply should convey. Meanings, not wordings.

  **This is where these annotations go wrong, so read the rest of this carefully.** Require only what the *vocalization* makes necessary — the semantic or pragmatic adaptation a reply has to make because that sound was there rather than nothing. Do not require a next action. Do not require a topic. Do not require a detail the reply could sensibly leave out.

  A real failure. The final turn was *"(gasps) The ball just came straight into the camera. Dad threw it right at whoever was filming."* One guide required the reply to convey:

  > Keeps attention on the tape — rewind, replay, or watch what happens next

  and another required:

  > Adds something of her own — memory of Dad's throwing, or wanting to keep/show it

  Now consider a reply: *"Whoa, that came out of nowhere."* It hears the gasp, it takes up the surprise, it is exactly what a person says. Under those requirements it scores badly for not proposing a replay and not volunteering a memory — neither of which the gasp obliges anyone to do.

  What the gasp does oblige is treating the moment as startling rather than ordinary. That is the requirement. Everything past it is one of several natural continuations, and belongs in `acceptable_variations` if it belongs anywhere.

  So before writing each requirement, ask: **would a reply that omits this have failed to understand the sound?** If not, it is not required.
- `acceptable_variations` — two natural continuations that are fine but not required. Anything you were tempted to put in `required_content` because it would be a good reply, rather than because the sound demands it, belongs here.
- `must_avoid` — one or two ways a reply would go wrong under this interpretation specifically. Ways of misreading the sound, not ways of being less thorough than you would have been.
- `example` — one brief natural reply, for human inspection only. Judges are told never to require similarity to it.

CONSTRAINTS

- Describe what the reply should do, not how it should sound. Delivery is scored separately.
- Do not require particular words or phrases. Several natural replies should satisfy any one guide.
- The reply addresses the conversation. It does not analyse the sound out loud — "you sound relieved" is a bad reply, not a good one.
- It should sound like the person who has been in this conversation, not a therapist, an interviewer, or a customer-service agent.
- Do not reward a reply merely for being on topic.
- Where the speaker may be hurt, frightened or in distress, the right reply does not have to mirror that. It may steady the situation instead — checking, calming, offering something concrete.
