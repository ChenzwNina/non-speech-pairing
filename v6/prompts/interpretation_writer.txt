You are writing reference annotations for a benchmark on whether spoken-language models understand what a non-speech vocalization means in conversation.

You are given one five-turn conversation between two speakers. In the final turn, the speaker produces a single non-speech vocalization, and you are told which one it is. That label is authoritative — you are not being asked to detect it.

Your task is to write the interpretations of that vocalization this conversation actually supports. There may be one, two or three. Do not pad the list to reach three.

WHAT THESE ARE FOR

A model under test will be asked, in its own words, what the vocalization expresses. Its answer will be compared against your three interpretations, and it passes if it matches any one of them. So these are the boundary of what counts as understanding this item — not one right answer with two wrong ones.

Every interpretation must be a reading a competent listener could hold after hearing this exchange. If you would argue against one, it does not belong in the list.

**One well-supported interpretation is a better annotation than three where the third is invented.** Padding does real damage: a model that responds naturally and correctly gets marked down for failing to address a reading the conversation never carried.

Here is a real failure. The final turn is *"(gasps) The ball just came straight into the camera. Dad threw it right at whoever was filming."* Two readings hold up — startled by the ball at the lens, and delighted at what the old tape caught. A third was added:

> Startled and moved, dawning realization about the unseen person holding the camera.

The transcript says *whoever was filming*. That phrase says the speaker does **not** know who held the camera, and nothing in the five turns makes the camera-holder's identity the point. The third reading was inferred to fill a slot, and the response guide built on it then required a reply to engage with who was filming — so a reply that simply reacted to the ball would lose marks for missing something that was never there.

Ask of each interpretation: **which words in these five turns support this?** If the answer is a phrase you have to read past its plain meaning, leave it out.

Where there is genuine spread, cover it — the obvious reading, a reading emphasising a different aspect of the speaker's state, a mixed or hedged one. Where there is not, say so by writing fewer.

Each interpretation has two parts, and both are required:

- `reading` — what the speaker is feeling or doing. The speaker's state.
- `interactional_function` — what the sound accomplishes in the exchange. What it does to the conversation, not what it feels like.

A reading without a function is incomplete: "amusement" is not an interpretation, "amused disbelief that the employer wants yet another interview" paired with "frames the extra interview as somewhat absurd" is.

THE SHARED IMPLICATION

Then state what every interpretation in your list agrees on. This is the line a wrong answer crosses — a model whose reading contradicts the shared implication has misunderstood the item, even if it sounds confident. Write it as what the sound is *not* doing, or what it commits the speaker to, whichever is sharper.
