You are planning a contrastive stimulus for a benchmark on whether spoken-language models hear meaning in non-speech vocalizations.

A five-turn conversation between two speakers will be written. The very same words will be heard three ways: once with no non-speech vocalization at all, and twice with one vocalization at the end of the final turn — a different one each time.

You are not writing the dialogue yet. You are deciding what it should be about, and what each of the two vocalizations would make it mean.

YOUR TASK

Given a situation and two vocalizations, work out a five-turn exchange in which BOTH vocalizations are things the final speaker could genuinely produce, and in which each one sends the whole conversation somewhere different.

THE REQUIREMENT THAT MATTERS

The two emotional framings must be genuinely different — not two shades of the same feeling, and not the same stance at two intensities. Different valence, or a different appraisal of what has happened, or a different stance toward the other speaker.

These are too close, and would fail:
- "mildly frustrated" against "quite frustrated"
- "amused" against "delighted"

These are far enough apart:
- the speaker is letting the whole thing go as funny, against the speaker conceding it is not going to change
- the speaker has just realised something they had not seen, against the speaker is hurt by what they have realised

Both framings must be honest readings of the SAME five turns. If you find yourself needing the words to differ, the plan is wrong — go back and choose a situation where one set of words genuinely supports both.

WHAT MAKES THIS POSSIBLE

The vocalization lands at the end of the conversation, so it reinterprets what has already been said rather than colouring what comes next. Plan for that: the exchange should build to something whose meaning is still open when the last speaker reacts to it. A conversation that has already settled how everyone feels leaves the sound nothing to do.

Do not let the words name the emotion. If a line says "I'm so relieved" or "that's hilarious", the sound is redundant and the item is dead. The words report what happened; the sound says how the speaker is taking it.

USING THE SITUATION

The situation comes from EmpatheticDialogues and is a seed, not a script. Take the circumstance and the relationship. Do not reproduce its wording, and do not name the emotion it was filed under.

IF IT CANNOT BE DONE

If this situation genuinely cannot support both vocalizations — not that it is hard, but that one of them would be incoherent or callous here — set `feasible` to false and say why. A forced item is worse than a missing one.
